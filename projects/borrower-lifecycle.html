Understanding when borrowers return

I analyzed the journey from a first loan to repeat borrowing, and from customer activation to booking. By combining survival analysis with cohort-based conversion curves, I examined both customer participation and the timing of conversion.

FTB to repeat borrower

Measuring the path to a second loan

I focused on first-time borrowers with matured loans and follow-up time after closure. I used Kaplan–Meier survival analysis to account for borrowers whose observation ended before a second loan was seen.

45.8%observed second-loan conversion in the survival dataset
6.7 monthsestimated time for 25% of the population to repeat, measured from first booking
15.5 monthsestimated time for 50% to repeat, measured from first booking

The curve estimates the share yet to take a second loan. At the 15.5-month crossing, only 12.58% of the starting population remained at risk, so the tail is less stable.

How to read the curve

A steeper decline means second-loan events are occurring more quickly. The curve accounts for unequal follow-up; it does not prove why a customer returned or establish that those not yet observed will never return.

Two starting points

First booking and loan maturity answer different questions

Time from first booking

This captures the full customer journey, including the time the first loan is active. The estimated 25% conversion milestone was approximately 6.7 months.

Time from maturity

This focuses on the post-maturity period. The estimated 25% milestone was approximately 1.4 months. Maturity was used as the analysis anchor, not as proof of individual eligibility.

Maturity-based calculations set negative elapsed times to zero. These two views use different clocks and should be interpreted separately.

Business application

Lifecycle milestones can inform when to investigate re-engagement, compare borrower cohorts and evaluate whether a strategy changes the timing or incidence of repeat borrowing.

Activation & booking velocity

Separating exposure time from conversion pace

I investigated a September production slowdown by comparing first-time and repeat borrowers at the same activation age. September’s first-half activation began four calendar days later than August’s.

First-time borrowers: August and September compared at the same activation age.

Repeat borrowers: August and September compared at the same activation age.

What I found

Both FTB and RB September cohorts converted more slowly than August and the historical same-age benchmark. The later start also shortened the available exposure window. These findings point to customer mix, eligibility and offer structure as areas for further investigation.

Analytical approach

Making the comparison consistent

Aligned cohorts

I assigned bookings to their activation window and aligned the booking numerator with the whitelist population and deciles represented in the denominator.

Comparable exposure

I compared cohorts at matching relative days and restricted historical benchmarks to cohorts observable through the full comparison horizon.

Timing scenario

I estimated the historical gain from four more exposure days using each eligible cohort’s own Day 12 to Day 16 increase. The result is directional given the small historical sample.

For a separate production view, I compared August and September through the same calendar cutoff. I also developed a marketable-age calculator to examine age distributions and booking rates by borrower type, risk decile and activation period.

Business value

Turn timing into a better question

This work connects customer lifecycle behavior to activation performance. It helps teams distinguish a shorter opportunity window from a change in booking pace, and gives them a clearer basis for investigating retention and conversion.
