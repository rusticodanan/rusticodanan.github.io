Does borrowing speed signal repayment risk?

I used survival analysis to explore how the time between repeat loans relates to the timing of 30+ days past due. The analysis connects repeat-borrower growth with a closer look at repayment behavior.

The business question

The early repeat window: growth and risk

Repeat borrowing creates opportunities to deepen customer relationships. I wanted to understand whether borrowers who return sooner also show a different time-to-delinquency pattern, and where that relationship is strongest.

The approach

From loan history to a survival dataset

01 · Measure the gap

I ordered each customer’s loans by booking date and measured the time between consecutive bookings in months. First loans were excluded because there was no previous booking to compare.

02 · Define the event

I identified the first observed month on book with a positive 30+ DPD end balance. Loans without an observed event were treated as censored in the model.

03 · Compare hazard

I fitted Cox models using a continuous gap, alternative gap buckets and loan-sequence groups. I also used Kaplan–Meier curves to visualize time without an observed 30+ DPD event.

What a hazard ratio means

A hazard ratio compares the modeled instantaneous event rates among loans still at risk at a given time. It is not a difference in absolute delinquency probability.

Finding 01 · Continuous gap

Longer gaps were associated with lower hazard

In the single-variable Cox model, each additional month between bookings was associated with an estimated 9.1% lower instantaneous hazard of reaching 30+ DPD, under the model assumptions.

0.909hazard ratio per additional month
0.901–0.917approximate 95% confidence interval for the hazard ratio
0.552in-sample concordance index

The relationship is useful for understanding portfolio behavior, while the concordance result shows that this one-variable model has limited ability to rank loans by event timing on its own.

Finding 02 · Gap buckets

Risk differs across the early repeat window

I compared distinct booking-gap groups with borrowers whose gap was greater than six months. Each ratio describes the group’s modeled hazard relative to that reference.

Reading the pattern

The relationship is not a simple progression in which the shortest gap always has the highest hazard. The 1–6 month groups each show higher modeled hazards than the >6-month reference. I also explored a spline formulation to allow for a nonlinear relationship.

Finding 03 · Loan sequence

Does experience change the relationship?

I fitted separate single-variable Cox models by loan sequence to see whether the association between an extra month’s gap and delinquency timing differed across borrower journeys.

Second loan0.872

Fifth loan onward0.939

The estimated association varies by loan sequence; the fifth-and-later group has the ratio closest to 1. The sequence estimates do not decrease monotonically, so a smooth trend across every loan number should not be assumed.

Business application

Use velocity as a signal to investigate

The analysis gives lending and risk teams a way to consider borrowing speed alongside customer experience and repayment behavior. It can inform further segmentation, monitoring and testing of risk-aware engagement strategies during the early repeat window.

Next steps for validation

The models above use inter-loan gap alone and show association rather than causation. Before using the signal operationally, I would validate censoring against each loan’s actual observed follow-up, assess proportional hazards and repeated loans per customer, add relevant risk controls, and test performance on later cohorts. The notebook assigns non-event duration to loan term, which can overstate follow-up for loans not observed through maturity.

Connecting growth and risk

Understanding when repeat borrowers return is one part of the customer story. Understanding how repayment risk evolves adds another dimension to the decisions teams make about customer growth.
