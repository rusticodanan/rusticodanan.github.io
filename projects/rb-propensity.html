Predicting the Next Borrower.

I developed a repeat borrower propensity framework to rank marketed customers by their likelihood of booking another loan. The work combines historical lending behavior, customer lifecycle information, marketing exposure, and financial-flow signals to support smarter lead prioritization.

I modeled repeat borrowing using a unified framework across FTB-to-RB1 and established RB-to-RB customers, then assessed ranking separately within each segment. Chronological development windows were used instead of random row splits to keep historical campaign cycles ordered.

Study preparation

Audited 1.29 million source rows and 196 columns, checked lead-cycle uniqueness and target quality, applied the finalized-cohort rules, and reduced the clean modeling population to about 1.18 million records.

Target and validation

The modeled outcome was whether a marketed repeat-borrower lead booked. The initial modeling windows used training through February 2026, validation from March through May, and a later June–August evaluation period.

Exploratory data analysis

The patterns that shaped the model

Conversion varies by borrower stage

16.19%FTB → RB1 booking rate
33.03%Established RB → RB booking rate

Established repeat borrowers converted more frequently than first-time borrowers approaching their second loan. This supported segment-aware ranking and measurement.

Borrowing recency matters

Recent historical borrowers were much more likely to book again. In the saved EDA, booking rates were approximately 73.0% for a 0–30-day recency bucket, 62.8% for 31–60 days, and 56.5% for 61–90 days.

Unadjusted cohort associations, not a causal estimate of the effect of recency.

Marketing history signals fatigue

Prior lead cycles and past unsuccessful marketing contacts helped distinguish repeat prospects from repeatedly marketed non-bookers. Time since previous marketing and historical no-book cycles emerged as important predictors.

Cash flow adds context

I examined historical salary credits, non-salary credits, debits, transaction activity, and changing financial-flow patterns. The payroll-specific experiment extended this analysis with engineered recent-versus-older cash-flow dynamics.

Key EDA interpretation

Conversion was not uniform across the marketable RB pool. Lifecycle stage, booking recency, and prior marketing history offered clearer ways to distinguish high- and low-propensity leads.

Payroll-focused challenger

For the payroll-only analysis, I expanded the feature set with financial-flow measures and compared model families again. The validation-selected model was XGBoost, with ROC-AUC approximately 0.8754 and average precision approximately 0.6981.

Later-period performance

The initial unified LightGBM model achieved a recorded 0.8702 ROC-AUC on the later June–August test period. In the subsequent challenger notebooks, this same period was revisited, so I treat it as a later-period benchmark rather than a wholly untouched final holdout across all iterations.

Interpretation

Recent, responsive customers tend to look different from stale or repeatedly unconverted leads. Eligibility age, previous campaigns, time since marketing, and time since borrowing give the model a stronger view of customer readiness than demographic information alone.

Results and business interpretation

From prediction scores to actionable ranking

Segment-specific prioritization

For FTB-to-RB1 customers, the general CatBoost challenger ranked the highest-propensity decile at 62.8% observed booking rate in the later-period analysis, compared with the overall FTB-to-RB1 booking rate of approximately 15.3% in that period.

Deciles are within-segment rankings; the figures describe observed results, not a guarantee of future conversions.

Operational interpretation

I designed the scores to support differentiated marketing intensity, campaign allocation, and prospect prioritization. A separate ranking for first-time-to-repeat and established repeat borrowers avoids letting their different baseline conversion rates distort a shared operational queue.

Model diagnostics

I reviewed ROC and precision–recall performance, calibration, decile reliability, PSI, and feature-level explanations to understand whether ranking strength held up beyond the development data.

What I learned

Improved prioritization depends on combining lifecycle knowledge with time-aware validation. High-performing classification metrics matter, but segment capture, score stability, and actual campaign constraints determine how useful a propensity model is.
