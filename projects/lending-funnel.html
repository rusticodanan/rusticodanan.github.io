Why this matters

Increasing applications alone would not address a bottleneck at an early screening step. I used the rule-level view to focus the discussion on eligibility design and the characteristics of the population reaching each checkpoint.

Reapplication behavior

Customers returned, but often met the same barrier

About 17.9% of the roughly 16.8k customers reapplied. Among reapplications after a savings-utilization rejection, 2,931 of 3,084 failed that same rule again.

Reapplication timing

In the reapplication-gap distribution, 39% occurred after seven days and another 39% after eight to ten days. The recurring window suggests customers remained interested in borrowing.

78% across the displayed 7-day and 8–10-day groups; category percentages are rounded.

Same-rule failures

About 95% of reapplications after a savings-utilization rejection failed the same rule. Reapplication count is the denominator for this measure; it is different from the unique-customer count.

2,931 same-rule failures / 3,084 reapplications.

The customer story

People were willing to try again, but a repeat application did not necessarily change the underlying condition that caused the first rejection. That creates an opportunity to make eligibility requirements clearer and guide customers toward the next appropriate action.

The presentation’s journey example shows more time after approval than in decisioning. Offer attractiveness, customer hesitation and booking-step experience are possible explanations to investigate. With only nine approvals and one booking, the timing and abandonment observations are highly sensitive to individual cases.

Rule-level analysis

Understand what reapplication can and cannot change

Savings utilization

I examined the underlying tenure, average balance and minimum end-of-day balance components to see how far rejected applicants were from the criteria.

Loan purpose

Of 20 customers who reapplied after a loan-purpose failure, 16 changed the purpose and did not fail that rule again. All 16 subsequently failed other rules, so changing that input alone did not lead to approval.

Decisioning order

Five of seven customers who reapplied after a TransUnion rejection failed the same check again. The other two dropped out earlier on reapplication, so their later TransUnion outcome was not observed.

Analytical approach

From stage counts to customer behavior

Map the journey

I reconstructed the sequential application funnel and measured the number remaining after each rule.

Follow repeat attempts

I linked earlier and later applications, measured the time between attempts and compared the rules failed across attempts.

Investigate thresholds

I grouped rejected applicants by distance from selected thresholds and examined whether mutable inputs changed across applications.

Turning analysis into action

The combined view helps teams prioritize where to investigate eligibility criteria, improve customer communication and assess the post-approval journey. Proposed changes should be tested against conversion and portfolio risk together.

Making the funnel actionable

The project connects application-level rules to the actual customer journey, making it easier to see both where volume is lost and why repeated attempts may lead to the same outcome.
